Chapter One · The Number

Forty is what
people say when
they mean
long enough.

Start with the strangest fact about the number forty, which is that nobody chose it. It simply keeps appearing, in cultures that had no contact with one another, at exactly the same moment in the story: the interval between who someone was and who they are about to become.

In the Hebrew scriptures it rains for forty days. Moses is on the mountain for forty days, twice. A generation walks for forty years. Elijah travels for forty days on a single meal. Nineveh is given forty days. The number is never presented as a measurement. It is presented as a duration of a particular kind, the kind you do not come back from unchanged.

Then it escapes religion entirely. When the plague reached the Adriatic, the Great Council of Ragusa ordered arriving ships held in isolation for thirty days. That was in 1377, and it was called the trentino. It was not enough. Over the following decades the ports of the Mediterranean pushed the period out to forty days, the quarantena, and the practice took the number as its permanent name. We still call it quarantine, and almost nobody using the word knows they are saying forty.

Forty days of confinement after birth is observed, independently, in Latin America, the Levant, the Balkans and East Asia. Forty days of mourning is kept in Islam and in Orthodox Christianity. Forty days appears in Hippocratic medicine as the span of a critical illness. Different centuries, different continents, no shared source, one number.

Which suggests something less mystical and more interesting. Forty is roughly six weeks. It is long enough that you cannot hold your breath through it, and short enough that a person can picture the far end of it. It is the smallest span that cannot be faked.

Chapter Two · The Fast

Forty days
before a single
word in public.

The best known forty in the world belongs to a man who spent it doing nothing that could be seen. In Matthew and in Luke, Jesus goes into the wilderness immediately after his baptism and fasts for forty days and forty nights. Only afterwards, hungry, does he meet the three propositions that the accounts record: turn these stones into bread, throw yourself down and be caught, take the kingdoms of the world and their glory.

Read them as a set and they are not three random temptations. They are the three ways a person with power uses it on himself. Feed yourself first. Prove yourself publicly. Take what is available because it is available. Each is refused. Then the public work begins, and every recorded act of it runs the other way: the food goes to the crowd, the healing goes to strangers, the towel and the basin go to the feet of the people who were meant to be serving him.

The forty days are not the achievement. They are the preparation that makes the direction of the next three years possible. Whatever a reader believes about the rest of it, the structure is unmistakable and worth borrowing: a long private emptying, and then a life spent pointed outward.

It is the same shape you find in the other forties. Nobody emerges from one with a new opinion. They emerge with a new direction.

Nobody comes out of forty days with a new opinion. They come out pointed a different way.

Chapter Three · The Evidence

What the research actually says, which is not what you have been told.

Here is where the story gets awkward for anyone selling a number of days, ourselves included.

The famous claim is twenty one days. It comes from a 1960 book called Psycho Cybernetics by Maxwell Maltz, a plastic surgeon, who observed that his patients seemed to need a minimum of about twenty one days to stop seeing a stranger in the mirror. That is an observation about adjusting to a new face. It is not a study, it has no control, and it is not about habits at all. Six decades of self help repetition sanded off the qualifier and the context, and left a rule.

The real work was done at University College London and published in 2010. Phillippa Lally and colleagues followed ninety six people who each chose one new daily behaviour and reported every day for twelve weeks on whether they had done it and how automatic it felt. Fitting a curve to each person's data, the median time to reach ninety five per cent of their own plateau of automaticity was sixty six days. The range ran from eighteen days to two hundred and fifty four.

Two findings from that paper matter more than the headline number. The first is the spread. A range of eighteen to two hundred and fifty four days means there is no universal duration, and any product that promises one is selling you an average you may not be near. The second is quieter and more forgiving: missing a single day did not materially damage the process. The curve tolerated lapses. What it did not tolerate was inconsistency of context.

Later evidence has held that shape. A 2024 systematic review pooling twenty studies and more than two and a half thousand participants concluded that durable health habits typically consolidate over two to five months, and that automaticity accrues gradually rather than switching on at a threshold. In the gym literature, the useful predictor is not elapsed time at all. It is the number of sessions and the regularity of the cue.

So no, there is no study showing that forty days makes or breaks a habit. There is no study showing that any number of days does. Days were never the mechanism. Repetitions were, and days were just the container we happened to count them in.

The choice

So we kept the
number and threw
away the calendar.

Forty acts. Because an act is a repetition, and repetitions are the thing that actually accumulates. A day can pass without you. An act cannot.

A challenge measured in days has a failure mode built into it. Miss day nine and you have broken something, and the honest response is to start again on Monday, which is how most people quietly stop. Lally's data says the lapse never mattered. The design says it did. The design was wrong.

A challenge measured in acts cannot be broken, only paused. Nothing resets. Act nine sits there completed whether it happened last Tuesday or last year, and act ten is available whenever you are. You do not need momentum, discipline, or a good month. You need one act, forty times, in any order, at any pace, for the rest of your life if that is what it takes.

And unlike a habit, which is a thing you do to yourself, every one of these lands on somebody else. That is the part that changes the person doing it. Giving is not a virtue you practise until it takes hold. It is the essence of the thing: the only reliable route out of the small closed room of your own concerns is a door that opens outward, and it opens once per act.

Forty acts. Take them in any order, at any speed.

Movement One · The Near

The people already in your life. The hardest ten, because they can tell when you are performing.

  1. 01Write the letter you have been meaning to write.To the person who taught you something you still use every week. Post it. Do not text it.
  2. 02Take the chore nobody claims.The one that reappears every week without a name attached to it. Take it permanently.
  3. 03Call the relative you avoid because the call is long.Let it be long.
  4. 04Cook for someone who is tired.Deliver it hot, leave, and do not stay to be thanked.
  5. 05Say the specific thing.Not that they are wonderful. The exact thing they did, and what it changed.
  6. 06Give one whole afternoon.To someone's move, repair, paperwork or clear out. The unglamorous hours are the ones that count.
  7. 07Settle a debt nobody is chasing.An apology you owe, a favour never returned, a thing you borrowed and kept.
  8. 08Teach what you know, once, for free.To one person who asked and could not pay for it.
  9. 09Sit with someone through the appointment they are dreading.You do not have to say anything useful.
  10. 10Drop the standing grievance.Forgive it privately and never bring it up again, including as a joke.

Movement Two · The Stranger

People you will not see again. No relationship to maintain, no credit to collect.

  1. 11Buy the meal of the person ahead of you.Leave before they turn around.
  2. 12Learn the name of someone who serves you daily.Then use it, every time, for the rest of the year.
  3. 13Give up the better position.The seat, the shorter queue, the closer space, the earlier slot.
  4. 14Carry something heavy for someone who did not ask.Ask once, then simply take the other end.
  5. 15Tip the amount that changes the day.Not the amount that satisfies the etiquette.
  6. 16Stop for the person on the roadside.Or make the call that gets someone to them.
  7. 17Offer the tool before it is requested.Jump leads, an umbrella, a charger, a lift, a phone.
  8. 18Write the review the small business reads at midnight.Name the person who served you.
  9. 19Give directions by walking them there.Ten minutes of your life, and they arrive.
  10. 20Defend someone who is not in the room.Especially when agreeing would be easier and cost you nothing.

Movement Three · The Unseen

Acts that cannot be traced back to you. This is where the challenge stops being about you.

  1. 21Give once with no name attached.To a person who will never have any way of finding out.
  2. 22Pay a stranger's overdue account.A school lunch balance, a pharmacy tab, a utility arrears, a library fine.
  3. 23Clean the shared space that is not yours to clean.The stairwell, the kitchen, the kit room, the verge.
  4. 24Restock what you did not empty.Paper, soap, milk, salt, printer ink, the first aid kit.
  5. 25Fix the broken thing in public.The gate, the light, the notice board, the loose paver.
  6. 26Take the litter home from a place you love.One bag. Every visit. Say nothing about it.
  7. 27Give something your body renews.Blood, plasma, or your name on a donor register.
  8. 28Leave money where it will be found.By someone who needs it, not somewhere it can be photographed.
  9. 29Deliver the groceries and do not knock.Text after you have driven away.
  10. 30Do the work and let someone else be credited.Watch what happens to them. That is the whole reward.

Movement Four · The Lasting

Acts with a tail. These take longer than a day and outlive the doing.

  1. 31Commit one hour a week for a year.To a single organisation. Consistency beats intensity, in giving as in everything.
  2. 32Fund the boring line item.Fuel, insurance, monitoring, rent, salaries. Nobody sponsors these and everything depends on them.
  3. 33Sponsor a person's training, not their crisis.Fees, tools, a licence, a first set of equipment.
  4. 34Make the recurring gift instead of the annual one.Small and monthly beats large and forgotten, because it can be planned against.
  5. 35Put someone in the room.Make the introduction that costs you a little leverage.
  6. 36Hire or mentor the person who keeps being passed over.For reasons that were never about the work.
  7. 37Leave a structure behind.A bench, a tree, a shelf of books, a small fund, a repaired path.
  8. 38Write it into your will.One line. It takes an afternoon and it outlasts you.
  9. 39Tell nobody about any of this.The whole way through. If it has been posted, it counts for someone else.
  10. 40Hand The Forty to one person and start them.Then never ask how far they got.

How it works

The rules are mostly about what we refuse to do.
