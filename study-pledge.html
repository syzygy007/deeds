Most of it, given away.

In 2010, the richest people alive started signing a strange promise: the majority of everything they had would go back out the door. The fine print is the interesting part. There is none.

The Giving Pledge

It began with a series of quiet dinners in 2009. Warren Buffett and Bill and Melinda Gates started inviting the wealthiest families in America to talk about a subject rich people almost never discuss out loud: what all of it is actually for. A year later, in June 2010, the idea had a name. The Giving Pledge asked the world's billionaires to publicly commit the majority of their wealth to philanthropy, during their lifetimes or in their wills.

Here is the strange part. There is no contract. No pooled fund, no board, no enforcement. A signer writes a letter explaining why, the letter is published, and that is the entire mechanism. More than two hundred and forty people from roughly thirty countries have signed. The archive of letters may be the most honest collection of writing about money that exists.

Buffett set the tone by pledging more than 99 percent of his fortune, and made a point of the math: his family, he wrote, would give up nothing they actually needed. The pledge reframed extreme wealth as a stewardship problem. Past a certain point, the money was never going to be spent. The only question was whether it would ever do anything.

The genius was never the money. It was making the promise out loud.

The pledge has a patron saint, and almost nobody knew his name for decades. Chuck Feeney, cofounder of Duty Free Shoppers, secretly gave away billions through Atlantic Philanthropies while flying coach and wearing a fifteen dollar watch. He believed in giving while living, finished the job in 2020 with roughly eight billion dollars out the door, and died in 2023 owning almost nothing. Buffett called him the model for the whole idea.

The newest signers are rewriting the pace. MacKenzie Scott signed in 2019 and has since moved billions in unrestricted gifts, often to organizations that had never seen a major grant, with no naming rights and no strings. Find people doing the work. Trust them. Get out of the way.

You do not need a billion dollars for any of this to apply to you. The real invention of the pledge was never the money. It was the decision to make the intention public, because a promise said out loud recruits the next promise. That is the same physics this platform runs on. A deed seen becomes a deed repeated.
